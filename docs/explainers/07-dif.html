Differential item functioning: when an item is unfair

  • Analyst

Three methods for asking whether an item behaves differently for two groups of equal ability — and one distinction that no statistic can make for you, which is why the tool calls this a screen rather than a verdict.

The question, and the question it is not

Differential item functioning asks: do two respondents of the same ability, drawn from different groups, have the same chance of answering this item correctly? If not, the item is measuring something besides the trait — something that varies by group.

It is emphatically not the question of whether the groups score differently overall. That is impact, and it is usually a fact about the world rather than a defect in the item. If one group has had less schooling, they will score lower on a well-constructed test, and every item will show a group difference in raw terms. Flagging every item in that situation would be useless.

So every method here conditions on ability first, and then compares groups. They differ in what they condition on and in what they can see.

The distinction the statistics cannot make

DIF tells you an item behaves differently. It cannot tell you whether that difference is bias. Consider a maths word problem set in a cricket context, flagged as favouring one group. Two readings:

  • Bias. Familiarity with cricket is irrelevant to mathematics, so the item is measuring something it should not, and it should be revised.
  • Legitimate difficulty. If the construct is explicitly “applying mathematics to everyday contexts” and cricket is a chosen context, the difference may be part of what is being measured.

Deciding requires knowing what the item is asking and what the test claims to measure — which no statistic in the report can see. IRTBoss states this in the notes attached to every DIF analysis, in words that appear verbatim in the output: “DIF results are a screen, not a verdict. An item flagged here behaves differently between groups conditional on the trait; deciding whether that difference is bias requires knowing what the item is asking, which no statistic in this report can see.”

Two shapes of DIF

The distinction between them decides which method can detect what.

Method one: Mantel–Haenszel

Stratify respondents by their observed total score. Within each stratum — people who scored the same — build a 2×2 table of group by right/wrong, and pool the odds ratios across strata:

α̂MH = ∑s (AsDs / ns) / ∑s (BsCs / ns) (1)

where As, Bs, Cs, Ds are the four cell counts in stratum s. An odds ratio of 1 means no DIF. ETS converts it to a difficulty-difference metric:

ΔMH = −2.35 · ln α̂MH (2)

The 2.35 puts the result on the ETS delta scale, where a positive value conventionally indicates the item is easier for the focal group. This feeds the A/B/C classification, which combines statistical significance with effect size — the taxonomy used operationally in large-scale testing:

CategoryBroad criterionOperational meaning
A — negligibleNot significant, or |ΔMH| smallUse the item
B — moderateSignificant with a moderate |ΔMH|Usable; prefer an A-category item if one is available
C — largeSignificant with a large |ΔMH|Review the content before use; strong candidate for revision

The exact numeric cut-points are conventions from the ETS procedure rather than derived constants; the tool applies the conjunction of a significance test and an effect-size band, and reports both components so you can apply your own bands if your programme uses different ones.

Its great strength: no model is fitted, so nothing about the model can be wrong. If your 2PL fit is questionable, Mantel–Haenszel is unaffected.

Its blind spot, and why it matters

Mantel–Haenszel is blind to non-uniform DIF. Look again at the right-hand panel of the figure: below the crossing point the focal group is favoured, above it the reference group is. The stratum-specific odds ratios point in opposite directions, and pooling them averages the two away — landing near 1. An item can be badly non-uniform and score a clean ETS A. This is not a subtle inefficiency; it is a whole category of unfairness that the operational standard cannot see, and it is the reason the next method is reported alongside rather than instead.

Method two: logistic regression

Swaminathan & Rogers’ approach fits a sequence of nested logistic models predicting the response from the matching score, the group, and their interaction:

Model 1: logit(P) = β0 + β1S
Model 2: logit(P) = β0 + β1S + β2G uniform DIF
Model 3: logit(P) = β0 + β1S + β2G + β3(S × G) non-uniform DIF (3)

with S the matching score and G the group indicator. A significant β2 indicates uniform DIF; a significant β3 indicates non-uniform DIF. That interaction term is exactly what Mantel–Haenszel cannot see, which is the reason both are reported.

The effect size, and why it governs

IRTBoss flags on the change in McFadden’s pseudo-R² between models, not on the p-value:

R²McF = 1 − ℓmodel / ℓnull, ΔR²McF = R²full − R²reduced (4)

The reason is the same one from Part 6: with a few thousand respondents the likelihood-ratio test will find statistically real differences far too small to matter to anyone. The p-value is reported next to the effect size, not instead of it. The effect-size convention follows the lordif literature.

Its weakness

Logistic-regression DIF shares Mantel–Haenszel’s reliance on the observed total score as the matching variable. That score is measured with error, and under substantial impact the error is correlated with group membership — which inflates the Type I error rate. In plain terms: when the groups genuinely differ a lot in ability, both observed-score methods flag items that are fine. The next method addresses precisely this.

Method three: IRT likelihood ratio

Thissen, Steinberg & Wainer’s method matches on the latent trait instead of a fallible observed score. For the studied item, fit two models:

  • Constrained: the item’s parameters are forced equal across groups.
  • Free: the item’s parameters may differ across groups.

and compare with a likelihood-ratio statistic:

G² = 2 ( ℓfree − ℓconstrained ) ~ χ²df (5)

with df equal to the number of parameters freed. Two advantages follow: the matching variable is the latent trait rather than a noisy score, and the result says which parameter differs — difficulty (uniform) or discrimination (non-uniform).

The anchor problem, and purification

To compare two groups’ item parameters at all, the two calibrations must be on a common metric, and that metric is established by the items assumed to be DIF-free — the anchor set. This creates a circularity: the anchors are supposed to be clean, but you do not know which items are clean until you have run the analysis.

If a contaminated item is left in the anchors, the two groups are linked on a crooked metric and the contamination is smeared across every other item — you get diffuse, misleading flags everywhere rather than a clean flag on the culprit.

The remedy is a purification loop:

  1. Run DIF with all items as anchors.
  2. Remove the flagged items from the anchor set.
  3. Re-run with the reduced anchor set.
  4. Repeat until the flagged set stops changing.

IRTBoss implements this and reports the anchors actually used, which is essential: an IRT-LR result without its anchor set is uninterpretable, because a different anchor set can give a different answer on the same data.

A limitation specific to this implementation

The IRT-LR method here fits both groups under a single latent population distribution, so it is approximate under substantial group impact — exactly the situation where you reached for it over the observed-score methods. Where impact is large, the observed-score methods are the ones to trust, and the limitation is documented in the module and in PROGRESS.md. Reading this correctly matters: it means the three methods do not form a hierarchy with IRT-LR at the top. They form a triangulation.

Multiple comparisons

A 40-item test tested for DIF is 40 hypothesis tests. At α = 0.05 you expect two false flags from noise alone, and a reviewer will reasonably ask about it.

IRTBoss applies the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure across items, controlling the false discovery rate rather than the family-wise error rate. That is the right choice for a screening context: FDR control accepts a known proportion of false positives among the flagged set in exchange for far more power than Bonferroni, and since DIF flags lead to content review rather than to automatic removal, a false positive costs a reviewer’s time rather than a wrong decision.

Benjamini–Hochberg in one step

Sort the m p-values ascending as p(1) … p(m). Find the largest k such that

p(k) ≤ (k / m) · q

and reject all hypotheses up to k. Here q is the target false discovery rate. Note the contrast with Bonferroni, which compares every p-value against q/m — the strictest possible bar, applied uniformly.

Reading the three methods together

They are reported together because each is blind to something the others see. The disagreement patterns are informative in themselves:

A practical workflow

  1. Check impact first. Note the ability difference between groups. Large impact changes how much you trust the observed-score methods.
  2. Read the ETS categories. C-category items are your review list. But do not stop there.
  3. Read the logistic interaction terms for anything MH missed. This is the step most often skipped and the one that catches the invisible category.
  4. Read IRT-LR with its anchor set. If the anchor set is small or the impact large, weight it less.
  5. Rank by effect size, not by p-value, and note the sample size.
  6. Take the flagged items to someone who knows the content. This is the step the statistics exist to serve, and it is not optional — DIF is a screen, not a verdict.

What you now know

  • DIF conditions on ability then compares groups; impact — a raw group difference — is usually a fact about the world, not a defect.
  • Uniform DIF shifts a curve; non-uniform DIF crosses curves. Mantel–Haenszel averages the crossing case away and can score badly non-uniform items as clean.
  • MH needs no model, uses the ETS A/B/C conjunction of significance and effect size, and is the operational standard.
  • Logistic regression adds the interaction term MH cannot see, flags on ΔMcFadden R², and inherits observed-score matching error under large impact.
  • IRT-LR matches on the latent trait and identifies which parameter differs, but depends entirely on its anchor set — hence purification, and hence the anchors are reported.
  • This implementation of IRT-LR assumes a single latent population, so it is approximate under substantial impact; prefer the observed-score methods there.
  • Benjamini–Hochberg controls the false discovery rate across items, which fits a screening context.
  • No statistic can distinguish bias from legitimate difficulty. That requires reading the item.
