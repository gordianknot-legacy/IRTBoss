Why measuring people is harder than counting answers

  • Stakeholder
  • Analyst

Everything in this series follows from one observation: the number of questions someone got right is not a measurement of anything, and treating it as one quietly breaks decisions that matter.

Start with a spreadsheet

Suppose you gave a ten-question maths test to four hundred students. You have a grid: one row per student, one column per question, a 1 where the answer was correct and a 0 where it was not. This is where every analysis in IRTBoss begins, and it is worth being precise about how little it tells you on its own.

The obvious thing to do is add up each row. Aisha got 7, Ben got 7, Chandra got 4. You now have a ranking, and rankings feel like measurements. They are not, and the gap between the two is the entire subject of this series.

The first problem: a total ignores which questions

Aisha and Ben both scored 7. But suppose Aisha answered the three hardest questions correctly and missed three easy ones, while Ben answered every easy question and missed the three hardest. Their totals are identical. Almost nobody would conclude that their mathematics is identical.

You might object that Aisha’s pattern is odd — why would a strong student miss easy questions? — and you would be right to notice. That oddness is itself information, and Part 6 shows how the tool measures it as person misfit. The point here is narrower: the total score threw the information away before anyone could look at it.

The second problem: the number depends on the test

Scoring 7 out of 10 means something different on an easy test than on a hard one. So a sum score is not a property of the student; it is a property of the student and the particular questions they happened to face. Change the questions and the number changes, even though nothing about the student changed.

This has consequences that show up immediately in practice:

  • You cannot compare across forms. If this year’s paper is slightly harder than last year’s, an apparent drop in scores may be entirely an artefact of the paper.
  • You cannot compare across groups given different items. Common in large surveys, where not every respondent sees every question.
  • You cannot measure growth. A pre-test and a post-test with different questions produce two numbers on two different, unknown scales.

The third problem: the scale is not evenly spaced

Going from 4 correct to 5 correct is not the same amount of improvement as going from 8 to 9. Near the middle of a test, one more correct answer is a small step; near the top, it can require a large gain in ability, because only hard items remain. The sum score is an ordinal scale masquerading as an interval one.

This matters the moment anyone does arithmetic on the numbers. Averages, gain scores, effect sizes and regression coefficients all assume that a one-unit difference means the same thing everywhere on the scale. On raw totals it does not.

Why this is not a pedantic point

Consider a programme evaluation that reports “the intervention raised scores by 1.2 points on average.” If most of the treated group started near the top of the test, 1.2 points represents a very large ability gain; if they started in the middle, a modest one. The same reported number supports opposite conclusions, and nothing in the number itself tells you which case you are in.

What classical test theory does, and where it stops

The traditional answer to all this is classical test theory (CTT), which models an observed score as a true score plus error:

X = T + E

where X is what you observed, T is the hypothetical true score, and E is measurement error with a mean of zero. From this, CTT derives reliability — the proportion of observed score variance that is true variance — and a standard error of measurement.

CTT is genuinely useful and still widely taught. Its limits are structural, not incidental:

Classical test theoryItem response theory
Item statistics (p-values, point-biserials) depend on the sample who took the testItem parameters are estimated on a scale that does not depend on the particular sample, given the model holds
Person scores depend on the particular itemsPerson estimates are on a common scale across different item sets
One reliability number for the whole testPrecision is a curve — high where the items are, low elsewhere
Standard error of measurement is a single constantStandard error varies by person, and is reported per person
No principled way to link two different testsEquating and linking follow directly from the common scale
Adaptive testing is not expressibleAdaptive testing is a natural consequence

The last row is the one that made IRT unavoidable in practice. If you want a test that chooses its next question based on how the candidate is doing — the mechanism behind the GRE, the GMAT, the military’s CAT-ASVAB and Duolingo’s English Test — you need a model that puts items and people on the same scale. CTT cannot express the question.

The idea that fixes it

Item response theory makes one structural assumption and follows it rigorously. The assumption: each person has a position on some underlying continuum — call it θ (theta) — and each item has its own properties, and the probability of a particular response is a function of both.

P(correct) = f(θperson, bitem, …)

Three things follow immediately, and they are worth stating slowly because they are the whole payoff.

  1. Items and people live on the same ruler. Because difficulty and ability enter the same equation in the same units, you can say “this item sits at 1.2 on the scale and this student sits at 0.8,” and the statement has content: the student will probably get it wrong.
  2. Getting a hard item right counts for more. The model does not add up correct answers; it asks which value of θ makes the observed pattern most probable. Aisha’s and Ben’s identical totals produce different estimates, because different patterns have different likelihoods under the model.
  3. Precision becomes local. Because probability varies smoothly with θ, the model can say how much a given set of items tells you at each point on the scale. A test packed with medium items measures the middle well and the extremes badly — and now you can quantify that instead of assuming one reliability everywhere.

The cost of these gains is that you are now committed to a model, and a model can be wrong. Most of this series — Parts 4 through 8 — is about that cost: how the parameters are estimated, how you check whether the model describes your data, and what to do when several models describe it about equally well.

What IRTBoss does with all this

IRTBoss takes the spreadsheet and returns a defensible analysis. Concretely:

The design premise: honesty before surface area

IRTBoss makes several choices that are unusual for analysis software, and they are consistent enough to state as a single premise: a number that cannot be defended should not appear. Four consequences you will meet throughout this series:

It refuses rather than repairs

If a column contains text where responses should be, the tool rejects the file and says which column. It does not helpfully convert the text to numbers by alphabetical order, which would silently permute an ordered rating scale and produce plausible, wrong results. Every refusal becomes a note that travels all the way to the report. See data-schema.md for the full list.

Non-convergence is fatal, not a warning

Iterative estimation sometimes fails to settle. Many tools return the last values they happened to reach, flagged with a warning that is easy to miss. IRTBoss returns no parameters at all and an explicit failure reason, at every layer — engine, orchestrator, worker and report. Part 4 explains why the intermediate values from a failed fit are not merely imprecise but meaningless.

It does not name a best model

Given several candidate models, the tool reports how each performs on several criteria, where the criteria disagree, and whether the models are statistically distinguishable at all on your sample. It does not output a best_model field. Part 8 gives the four reasons, including the one that reverses most people’s intuition: with a few hundred respondents, competing models are frequently indistinguishable, and a tool that picks one anyway is manufacturing confidence.

Absence must not look like a value

If a statistic could not be computed, the report shows an explicit marker, not a blank cell and never a zero. A blank reads as “nothing to report”; a zero reads as a measurement. Both are lies about a computation that did not happen.

Where this came from

These are not abstract principles. The previous version of this tool fabricated parts of its report at the presentation layer, shipped a model-fitting worker that nothing ever called, and reported a recommended model chosen by a rule its own documentation did not describe. That audit — with file and line evidence — is v2/PROBLEMS.md, and it is the reason the current version is built in the order it is: the estimator and its validation first, the interface last.

What you now know

  • A sum score confounds the person with the test, discards which items were answered, and is not on an interval scale.
  • Classical test theory manages error but cannot separate item properties from person properties, cannot link tests, and cannot express adaptive testing.
  • IRT models the probability of each response as a function of a person parameter and item parameters, which puts both on one scale and makes precision local.
  • The price is a modelling commitment, which is why fit, assumptions and model comparison occupy most of this series.
