IRTBoss documentation

Explainers

A complete path from “I have a spreadsheet of test answers” to “I can defend every number this tool produces, and I can maintain the code that produces it.” Thirteen parts, read in order or by track.

IRTBoss is a platform for item response theory — a family of statistical models that estimate how difficult each question on a test is and how much ability each person has, from nothing but the pattern of right and wrong answers. It is the machinery behind national school assessments, medical licensing exams, patient symptom questionnaires and adaptive tests that get harder when you do well.

This series assumes no statistics beyond arithmetic to begin with, and ends with the full derivation of the estimation algorithm the tool implements. Every mathematical step is shown; every step is also explained in words first, so you can read the prose straight through and come back for the algebra.

The parts

  1. Part 1 · Stakeholder · Analyst Why measuring people is harder than counting answers

    What goes wrong when you add up correct answers, why a number needs a scale before it means anything, and the one design decision that shapes everything else in this tool: it refuses to name a best model.

  2. Part 2 · Stakeholder · Analyst A history of item response theory, 1904–today

    From Spearman and Thurstone through Lord’s normal ogive, Birnbaum’s logistic, Rasch’s separability, Samejima’s graded responses and Bock & Aitkin’s EM algorithm, to the software era. Includes the Rasch-versus-IRT schism, which you will meet in the wild.

  3. Part 3 · Analyst · Maintainer The models: seven ways to turn a response into a probability

    Logits, latent traits and item characteristic curves. Rasch, 1PL, 2PL and 3PL for right/wrong items; GRM, PCM and GPCM for partial credit and rating scales. Full formulae, and what every parameter means in plain language.

  4. Part 4 · Analyst · Maintainer How the numbers are actually found

    The heart of the series. Why you cannot simply maximise the obvious likelihood, what marginalisation fixes, and a complete derivation of the Bock–Aitkin EM algorithm over a quadrature grid — the exact method in backend/app/irt/em.py. Then standard errors, the delta method, priors, missing data, and why non-convergence is treated as fatal.

  5. Part 5 · Analyst Scoring people, and how precisely

    Three ways to place a person on the scale — EAP, MAP and WLE — and what each trades away. Then Fisher information, the conditional standard error curve, and reliability done properly, including why this tool will not report Cronbach’s alpha.

  6. Part 6 · Analyst Does the model actually describe the data?

    Item fit by S-X² and the Lord–Wingersky recursion, infit and outfit, RMSD. Global fit by M2 and RMSEA2. Then the assumptions underneath everything: unidimensionality by parallel analysis and bifactor indices, and local independence by Q3*.

  7. Part 7 · Analyst Differential item functioning: when an item is unfair

    Mantel–Haenszel with the ETS A/B/C classification, logistic-regression DIF, and IRT likelihood-ratio DIF with anchor purification. Multiple comparisons. And the distinction that matters most: impact is not bias, and no statistic can tell you which you have found.

  8. Part 8 · Stakeholder · Analyst Comparing models without declaring a winner

    Held-out predictive likelihood by k-fold cross-validation, AIC and BIC, the nested likelihood-ratio ladder, and the disagreement matrix. Why the tool ships a dossier instead of a recommendation, and the four reasons that reversed an earlier decision to do the opposite.

  9. Part 9 · Stakeholder · Analyst Where this is used, and where it should not be

    School assessment, licensure, health outcomes, psychological scales, adaptive testing and test equating — with the sample sizes each needs. Then an honest account of what IRTBoss is not built for.

  10. Part 10 · Maintainer The software: every moving part

    The full journey of one request, from a browser upload to a rendered report. FastAPI, PostgreSQL, Redis, RQ, object storage, Argon2 sessions, React. Why estimation never runs in the web process, why the database stores a storage reference instead of a path, and how the tests are tiered.

  11. Part 11 · Analyst · Maintainer A real analysis, read line by line

    An actual run of 400 respondents and 12 items through 2PL and Rasch, with the real output: parameters, standard errors, comparison verdict, fit statistics, DIF screen. What each number is, and what it would take to change the conclusion.

  12. Part 12 · Everyone Glossary and references

    Every term used in the series, defined in one line. Then the primary literature, organised by what it settles.

Two honest caveats about this series

Citations should be verified before you rely on them externally. Authors, years, titles and venues in Part 12 are given as accurately as I can state them; volume and page numbers are deliberately omitted where I am not certain, rather than guessed at. Check any reference you intend to cite in your own work.

These are HTML files, not a website. GitHub shows HTML source rather than rendering it, so read these from disk — open docs/explainers/index.html in a browser — or serve the directory over GitHub Pages if you want links to work online.
